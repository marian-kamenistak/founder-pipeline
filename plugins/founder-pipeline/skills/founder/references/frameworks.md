# Frameworks reference

Cheat sheets for the 5 core frameworks this skill uses. Read the one relevant to the current stage. Each is condensed to "what / when / how / pitfall."

---

## 1. Value Proposition Canvas (Strategyzer / Alexander Osterwalder)

**What:** Two-sided map that forces fit between what the customer is trying to do and what your offer provides.

**When earns keep:** in P1.S6 (offer), right after the cited pain evidence is in (P1.S2's problem evidence + intake + P1.S4's prediction data).

**Customer side (derived from cited evidence — quotes, named accounts, prediction indicators):**
- **Jobs** — what they're trying to get done (functional, emotional, social)
- **Pains** — frustrations, risks, obstacles
- **Gains** — outcomes and benefits they want

**Offer side:**
- **Pain relievers** — how you reduce / eliminate the pains
- **Gain creators** — how you produce the gains
- **Products & services** — what you actually deliver

**Fit test:** every pain has at least one pain reliever; every top-3 gain has a gain creator. Anything unmatched = either the offer is missing something or the customer doesn't actually care.

**Output format (2-column table):**

| Customer side | Offer side |
|---|---|
| Job 1: [statement] | Pain reliever 1: [...] |
| Pain 1: [statement] | Gain creator 1: [...] |
| Gain 1: [statement] | Product feature 1: [...] |

**Pitfall:** filling in offer-side from imagination rather than cited evidence. If the pain side has no quotes, accounts, or prediction indicators behind it, the canvas is fiction.

## 2. Lean Canvas (Ash Maurya)

**What:** One-page business model on 9 blocks. Tightened version of Business Model Canvas for early-stage offers.

**When earns keep:** in P6.S3 (synthesis), as the brief's one-pager. NOT as the deliverable itself.

**The 9 blocks:**

| Problem | Solution | Unique Value Prop | Unfair Advantage | Customer Segments |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top 3 pains | Top 3 features | Single, clear, compelling message | What can't be easily copied | Target customers |
| **Existing alternatives** | **Key metrics** |   |   | **Early adopters** |
| What they use now | One north-star | (UVP wraps) | (Advantage wraps) | Within the segment |

Bottom row: **Cost structure** | **Revenue streams** (full-width below)

**Pitfall (Lean Canvas as deliverable):** the 9-block dump is a wall of text nobody reads. Use it as a thinking exercise that compresses into the brief. Don't ship it as the brief.

**Compression rule:** every block ≤ 3 bullets. If you need a paragraph, the block isn't ready.

## 3. Positioning (April Dunford — "Obviously Awesome")

**What:** A 5-step exercise that produces a positioning statement based on competitive alternatives, not on imagination.

**When earns keep:** P1.S5 (competition). Most "positioning" exercises skip step 1, which is why most positioning fails.

**The 5 steps:**
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would the customer do if your offer didn't exist? Include "do nothing" and "internal hack." Anything they'd actually choose.
2. **Unique attributes** — what does only you have? (Capabilities, IP, distribution, relationships, deep expertise, integrations.)
3. **Value (enabled benefit)** — what does your uniqueness allow the customer to do, that alternatives don't? (Map attributes → value, not the other way around.)
4. **Who cares the most** — which customer segment values that enabled benefit so much that they'd pay a premium, ignore competitors, recommend you?
5. **Market category** — what frame of reference do you want the buyer to use when evaluating? (Picking the wrong category = losing the deal before the demo.)

**Positioning statement format:**
> For [who-cares segment], [offer name] is the [category] that [enabled value], because [unique attributes].

**Pitfall:** skipping step 1. "We don't have competitors" = you don't understand the alternatives the customer actually considers.

**Dunford's litmus:** if your positioning would apply equally to a competitor with the labels swapped, it's not positioning. It's marketing copy.

## 4. Monetizing Innovation (Madhavan Ramanujam)

**What:** Price-before-product methodology. Test willingness-to-pay in the design phase, not after launch.

**When earns keep:** P2.S3 (pricing). Most pricing skills produce tier tables; this one forces price discovery first.

**The 4 failure modes to flag:**
1. **Feature Shock** — too many features, customer overwhelmed, can't articulate the value, won't pay.
2. **Minivation** — innovation priced too low to fund itself. Looks like product-market fit, actually underpriced into bankruptcy.
3. **Hidden Gem** — strong value, marketing/sales never positioned it, never reached its market.
4. **Undead** — product nobody asked for, nobody buys. Should have died in concept.

**Price discovery — Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (4 questions, run on 10+ prospects before launch):**
1. At what price would this be **so expensive** that you wouldn't consider buying it?
2. At what price would this be **expensive but you'd still consider** buying it?
3. At what price would this be **a bargain — too good to refuse**?
4. At what price would this be **so cheap** you'd doubt the quality?

Plot the four curves; intersections = price floor and price ceiling.

**Pricing model picker (7 models):**
- Subscription (predictable revenue, retention focus)
- One-time (transactional, scalable but no LTV moat)
- Usage-based (aligns with value, hard to forecast)
- Hybrid (base subscription + usage)
- Freemium (acquisition machine, conversion challenge)
- Enterprise (custom, high-touch, high ACV)
- Partner share (you take % of partner's outcome)

**Pitfall:** "we'll figure out pricing later." Pricing is the most consequential decision in the stream. Figure it out before you build the product.

## 5. Strategic Narrative (Andy Raskin)

**What:** A 5-act narrative structure for sales decks, fundraising decks, and category-defining messaging. Used by the best B2B sales decks.

**When earns keep:** P6.S1 (GTM) — generates the narrative arc your sales motion runs on. Reused in the P6.S3 brief.

**The 5 acts:**

1. **Shift** — name a big change in the world. (Regulatory, technological, economic, behavioral.) The shift must be undeniable.
2. **Promised land** — describe the new state the customer wants to reach in this shifted world. Make it vivid.
3. **Stakes** — what happens if they don't get there? (Winners take all, laggards die, competitors that adapted are eating their lunch.)
4. **Magic gifts** — your offer's unique capabilities that make the promised land reachable. (Map to P1.S5 & P1.S6 — unique attributes + value prop.)
5. **Proof** — evidence that the magic works. (Case studies, cited customer quotes, traction data.)

**Output format (one-pager):**
- Shift: 1 paragraph + 1 cited shift indicator
- Promised land: 1 paragraph, vivid present tense
- Stakes: 1 paragraph, what failure looks like
- Magic gifts: 3 bullets, each tied to a unique attribute
- Proof: 3 bullets of evidence with names + numbers

**Pitfall:** leading with "Our product does X." Lead with the shift. The product enters as the answer in act 4, not the question in act 1.

## Bonus: Ship / Release / Launch trichotomy (RampStack)

**What:** Three distinct tiers of "going live." Don't conflate them.

**Ship** = code merged, internal demo, tested with one friendly customer end-to-end. No marketing. No public price.

**Release (Pilot)** = paid pilot cohort. Quiet announcement to a list. Refines the offer through real money + real feedback. Still no public marketing.

**Launch (Public)** = full marketing motion. Public pricing. Target volume. Press if relevant.

**Why it matters:** most stream deaths happen at the Ship → Public skip. The Pilot stage is where the offer survives or dies. Skip it at peril.
